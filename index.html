Beyond Science · An essay in knots

The Persian Carpet فرش ایرانیfarsh-e irāni (FARSH-eh ee-raa-NEE)

A Persian carpet is a garden you can roll up and carry. It holds a design that can take months to knot by hand, a palette cooked from roots and rinds, and a history that runs back more than two thousand years. This page looks at the art behind it: where it came from, what its patterns mean, and why a good one lasts for generations.

"Every carpet is woven from the bottom up, one knot and one row at a time, so the weaver never sees the whole design until the carpet is finished." What the weaving process means in practice

I.

Two and a half thousand years of weaving

Pile carpets began as practical things: warm, portable floors for nomadic herders on the Iranian plateau. Over the centuries, royal courts turned them into one of the great decorative arts of the world.

  1. c. 400 BCE

    The Pazyryk carpet

    The oldest known knotted‑pile carpet was found in 1949, preserved in ice in a Scythian burial mound in the Altai Mountains of Siberia. It has a border of horsemen and grazing deer, and many scholars link its style to Achaemenid Persia. It is now in the Hermitage Museum in St Petersburg.

  2. 6th c. CE

    The Spring of Khosrow

    Written sources describe a huge Sasanian carpet in the palace at Ctesiphon. It showed a garden in bloom, worked in silk, gold and jewels, so the king could enjoy spring in winter. When Arab armies took the city in the 630s, the carpet was cut up and divided as spoils. None of it survives, but its garden theme continued for centuries.

  3. 11th–15th c.

    Seljuks, Mongols and Timurids

    Carpets show up in texts and in manuscript paintings from this period. Most designs were geometric: repeating stars, octagons and small medallions. Weaving spread across the plateau as a village and tribal craft.

  4. 1501–1722

    The Safavid golden age

    The Safavid shahs set up court workshops, and artists who normally illuminated manuscripts started designing carpets. Designs became curved and flowing, with medallions, arabesques and scrolling vines. The famous Ardabil carpet (1539–40), signed by Maqsud of Kashan, is now in the V&A in London. Under Shah Abbas I, Isfahan workshops wove silk carpets with gold and silver thread as diplomatic gifts for the courts of Europe.

  5. 18th c.

    From court to village

    Isfahan fell in 1722 and the court workshops declined. Weaving continued in towns, villages and nomadic camps, where designs were kept alive in memory and passed from mother to daughter.

  6. 19th c.

    The Qajar revival

    European demand brought a large revival. Merchants in Tabriz organised workshops for export, and foreign firms set up production around Sultanabad (Arak). Many classic Safavid designs were woven again, and new centres such as Kerman and Kashan became famous.

  7. 2010

    Recognised by UNESCO

    UNESCO added the traditional carpet‑weaving skills of Fars and of Kashan to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. In doing so it recognised the knowledge carried by weavers, dyers and designers, not only the finished objects.

II.

Reading a carpet

Most classical Persian carpets share the same parts. Once you know them, you can read almost any rug: a central field framed by borders, like a garden inside its walls.

  • Fieldمتنmatn (MATN)The main ground of the carpet. It can be an open colour, an all‑over repeat, or a scene.
  • Medallionترنجtoranj (toh-RANJ)The central motif, often read as a pool, a sun, or the dome of heaven. Designers draw one quarter and mirror it to make the whole.
  • Pendantsسرترنجsar-toranj (SAR-toh-ranj)Small caps at the top and bottom of the medallion that stretch it along the carpet's length.
  • Corner piecesلچکlachak (lah-CHAK)Quarter medallions in the four corners. They suggest that the design continues beyond the edges.
  • Bordersحاشیهhāshiyeh (haa-shee-YEH)A wide main border between narrower guard stripes. It works like a garden wall, and often has scrolling vines or palmettes.
  • Fringeریشهrisheh (ree-SHEH)Not decoration. These are the ends of the warp threads, the foundation that every knot is tied onto.
III.

A vocabulary of patterns

Persian weavers draw on a shared set of motifs, each with its own name and associations. Here are the ones you will see most often. Each photo is a close‑up of a real carpet, with a small sketch of the motif in the corner.

Lachak‑Toranj

لچک ترنجlachak-toranj (lah-CHAK toh-RANJ)

Medallion and corners. The classic "palace" layout, popular in Tabriz, Kashan and Kerman since Safavid times. A single centre gives the carpet a calm, balanced focus.

Detail: The Ardabil carpet, 1539–40, V&A London. Public domain.

Herati

هراتی · ماهیherāti · māhi (heh-raa-TEE · maa-HEE)

A rosette inside a diamond, with four curved leaves around it. Weavers also call it mahi (fish), because the leaves look like fish swimming around a pond. It repeats across the whole field, and is perhaps the most widely used Persian motif.

Detail: Bidjar rug, photo by BerndtF, CC BY-SA 3.0.

Boteh

بته جقهboteh jeqqeh (boh-TEH jeg-GEH)

A teardrop with a curled tip. It is known in the West as paisley, a name taken from the Scottish town that copied it. It has been read as a cypress bent in the wind, a flame, or a sprouting seed. It is common in Serabend and Qashqai rugs, and also in Kerman shawls.

Detail: Rug with rows of boteh, Rijksmuseum. CC0.

Shah Abbasi

شاه عباسیshāh abbāsi (shaah ab-baa-SEE)

Large fan‑shaped palmettes, named after Shah Abbas I. They are usually linked by scrolling vines (eslimi) that twist across the field. It is the typical motif of Isfahan, Kashan and Nain.

Detail: "Polonaise" silk carpet, Isfahan, 17th c., The Met. CC0.

Mina Khani

مینا خانیminā khāni (mee-NAA khaa-NEE)

A repeating lattice of daisy‑like flowers connected by thin stems. It spreads across the field with no clear beginning or end, and is a favourite of the Veramin weavers and of Kurdish weavers.

Detail: Varamin carpet, photo by Pouyakhani, CC BY-SA 3.0.

Garden

باغی · چهارباغbāghi · chahār-bāgh (baa-GHEE · cha-HAAR-baagh)

The Persian chahar bagh, a garden split into four by water channels, seen from above. It is the oldest idea in the tradition: paradise (from the Old Persian pairidaeza, a walled garden) laid on the floor. Bakhtiari "garden panel" rugs are a later version of it.

Detail: The Wagner garden carpet, Kerman, 17th c., Burrell Collection. Public domain.

Tree of Life

درخت زندگیderakht-e zendegi (deh-RAKHT-eh zen-deh-GEE)

A single tree rising from the base of the carpet with birds or blossoms in its branches, symbolising growth and the link between earth and sky. Cypresses (for eternity) and blossoming fruit trees (for renewal) often stand next to it.

Detail: Qashqai tree‑of‑life rug, Shiraz, photo by Hiart, CC0.

Mihrab

محرابیmehrābi (meh-raa-BEE)

A pointed arch based on the prayer niche of a mosque, sometimes with a lamp hanging from its peak. Prayer rugs have one arch and a clear direction: the weaver means them to be used facing one way.

Detail: Prayer rug, Iran, 16th c., The Met. CC0.

Gol‑o‑Bolbol

گل و بلبلgol‑o‑bolbol (GOL‑oh bol‑BOL)

"The rose and the nightingale". In Persian poetry, from Hafez to Saadi, the nightingale sings all night to a rose it can never have. It is the lover, and the rose is the beloved. Weavers fill the field with roses, vases of flowers and small birds on the branches. The motif was loved in Qajar painting and lacquer, and later in carpets.

Detail: Afshar carpet, gol‑o‑bolbol design on an ivory field.

Others you will meet: Harshang (crab‑like palmettes), Joshaghan (lozenges filled with flowers), Bid Majnun (the weeping willow), Shekargah (hunting scenes), Golfarang (European‑style bouquets from the Qajar period) and Kheshti (panel or brick layouts).

IV.

The craft, and why it holds up

A hand‑knotted carpet is a dense block of short wool tufts, each one tied around the foundation and held in place by weft threads beaten down tight. That structure is what lets it take a century of footsteps.

Two knots

Every pile knot wraps around two warp threads. How the yarn wraps decides the knot.

Asymmetrical · Persian Wraps one warp and passes under the other. One tuft comes up between each pair, which gives finer, more curved drawing.
Symmetrical · Turkish Goes over both warps, and both ends come up together in the centre. It is sturdy and suits bold designs. It is used in Tabriz, Heriz and Bidjar.

The Persian knot is also called Senneh, and the Turkish knot Ghiordes. Both are used inside Iran. The knot shows the local tradition, not the country of origin.

Wool, silk and natural dye

Highland sheep give wool rich in lanolin, which pushes away dirt and springs back under weight. The finest is kurk, from the neck and shoulders of young lambs. Silk gives a sheen and allows very fine detail, and city workshops use it for the pile or for highlights. Village rugs usually have a wool foundation, and city rugs a cotton one.

Natural dye batches never match exactly. The gentle bands of shifting colour this creates are called abrash, and collectors value them as a sign of the hand.

V.

Weaving as performance

A carpet is also something performed. In some workshops the design was sung rather than drawn.

 

In city workshops, a designer first paints the pattern onto graph paper called the naqsheh, where each square is one knot. In places such as Kerman, that chart was then turned into a talim, a coded list of colours and counts. A reader chanted it aloud to the weavers at the loom, who sang back each instruction as they tied it. The chant set the rhythm of the work and kept several weavers in step across a wide carpet.

Nomadic weavers, such as Qashqai women in Fars, often weave with no chart at all. They work from memory and improvise, and a small change of colour or an added animal records a birth, a season or a mood. Two carpets from the same camp are never the same.

After the loom, the work goes on: shearing the pile to an even height, washing, stretching and repairing. The last performance belongs to the owner. The pile wears into a soft sheen over the years, and a carpet that has been walked on for decades often looks better than a new one.

VI.

Where they are woven

Persian carpets are usually named after the town or tribe that made them. Each centre has its own palette, knot and style of drawing.

Tabriz تبریز

Northwest · city

A great trading centre that weaves almost every design at every quality. It often uses the symmetrical knot and a hooked tool called the qollab.

Heriz هریس

Northwest · village

Bold, angular medallions in brick red and navy. The thick, heavy build makes these among the toughest rugs made.

Kashan کاشان

Central · city

Home of Maqsud, weaver of the Ardabil carpet. Known for kurk wool, deep reds and navy, and for fine silk pieces. Listed by UNESCO.

Isfahan اصفهان

Central · city

The Safavid capital. Very fine knotting, often on silk foundations, with detailed Shah Abbasi designs and elegant medallions.

Nain نائین

Central · city

Ivory and soft blue grounds with silk outlines. A 20th‑century centre that came out of the town's fine wool textile tradition.

Qom قم

Central · city

Famous for all‑silk carpets that shine like enamel. Designs range widely, from gardens to boteh to hunting scenes.

Kerman کرمان

Southeast · city

Soft, painterly colours and naturalistic flowers, historically woven from a chanted talim. Kerman also has a long shawl tradition.

Bidjar بیجار

Kurdistan · town

Called the "iron rug of Persia". The wefts are beaten so hard that the carpet is stiff and very hard‑wearing.

Qashqai قشقایی

Fars · nomadic

Bright, spirited rugs woven on horizontal looms that can be carried from camp to camp, full of animals and small medallions. Listed by UNESCO.

Bakhtiari بختیاری

Zagros · village

Best known for the kheshti garden‑panel design, a grid of compartments each holding a tree, flower or vase.

VII.

Living with a Persian carpet

A carpet is made to be used. A little care lets it outlive the person who bought it.

Turn it

Rotate it every year or two so that sunlight and footsteps fall evenly. Strong direct sun will slowly fade even natural dyes.

Shake, don't scrub

Vacuum without a beater bar, and go with the pile. For spills, blot with cool water. Never rub, and never use harsh detergents on wool.

Guard the ends

The fringe and side cords wear first. Repair them early and the knots behind them stay safe.

Wash by hand

Every few years, a professional wash in plain water and a long dry in the shade brings the colour and lanolin sheen back.
